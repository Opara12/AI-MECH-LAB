Lesson
You now have both tools of supervised learning: regression for predicting numbers, classification for
predicting categories. This lesson puts them to work together in one applied project  end to end, from
raw data to a working prediction. You won't just read about the steps this time. You'll walk through
building something real.
The scenario
You're working with a small fleet of industrial motors, each fitted with basic sensors. For every motor, you
have three readings  runtime hours since last service, average load percentage, and vibration level 
plus two things you already know from maintenance records: the motor's current temperature, and
whether it eventually failed within 30 days of that reading.
That gives you everything you need for two separate models: a regression model to predict temperature,
and a classification model to predict fail/no-fail. Same data, two different kinds of questions.
One dataset, two different kinds of questions to answer.
Step 1  Look at your data before you touch a model
Every real project starts here, not with code. Your dataset looks like this:

--- PAGE BREAK ---

Three feature columns feeding two separate label columns.
Before building anything, ask what you'd ask of any dataset: which columns are features, and which is
the label? Here, runtime_hrs, load_pct, and vibration are your features for both tasks. temperature is the
label for the regression task. failed_within_30d is the label for the classification task. Same three inputs,
two different outputs  that's the whole structure of this project.
Step 2  Build the regression model: predicting temperature
The goal here is exactly what you covered in Topic 4.2  find the combination of weights across your
three features that produces a predicted temperature as close as possible to the real value, minimizing
error across the dataset.
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression
X = df[["runtime_hrs", "load_pct", "vibration"]]
y_temp = df["temperature"]
temp_model = LinearRegression()
temp_model.fit(X, y_temp)
new_motor = [[600, 89, 6.0]]
predicted_temp = temp_model.predict(new_motor)
# predicted_temp ~ 76.4°C

--- PAGE BREAK ---

Three features, one trained model, one predicted temperature.
Notice what's happening: fit() is the training step  the model searching for the best-fitting weights,
same idea as the slope-and-intercept line from Topic 4.2, just now across three inputs instead of one.
predict() is where you hand it a motor it hasn't seen and get a number back.
Step 3  Build the classification model: predicting failure
Same three features, different question. This time you're not predicting a number  you're predicting
one of two categories: will this motor fail within 30 days, or not.
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
y_fail = df["failed_within_30d"]
fail_model = LogisticRegression()
fail_model.fit(X, y_fail)
predicted_class = fail_model.predict(new_motor)
predicted_probability = fail_model.predict_proba(new_motor)
# predicted_class: "Yes"
# predicted_probability: [0.11, 0.89] -
> 89% confident it fails

--- PAGE BREAK ---

Same three features, a different model, a probability-based answer.
This is exactly the probability-based decision process from Topic 4.3  the model isn't just saying "Yes,"
it's saying it's 89% confident, with the underlying probabilities available if you want them.
Step 4  Put both predictions together
This is the payoff of doing both at once: for that same new motor, you now have two independent pieces
of information from the same three sensor readings  a predicted temperature of roughly 76
°
C, and an
89% chance of failure within 30 days.
Two independent predictions, from one shared set of sensor readings.

--- PAGE BREAK ---

Neither model knows about the other's output; each was trained on its own label. But used together, they
give you a far more complete picture than either one alone: not just "will it fail," but a sense of the
operating condition driving that risk.
Step 5  Sanity-check the predictions, don't just trust them
Before acting on either output, hold it up against what you already know. Does a predicted 76
°
C line up
with the pattern in the training data, where high runtime and high load consistently pushed temperature
into the 70s and 80s? Does an 89% failure probability line up with the fact that this motor's load and
vibration numbers sit close to the two motors in your training data that actually did fail?
A new prediction checked against the pattern already visible in the data.
If a prediction looks wildly out of step with the patterns you can see by eye, that's a signal to check your
data and your model before trusting the output  a habit worth building now, before the stakes get
higher.
