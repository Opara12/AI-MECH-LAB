Lesson
The last lesson gave you the vocabulary  AI, Machine Learning, Deep Learning, nested inside each other.
This lesson makes it concrete. Three areas where AI is actually changing how engineering gets done today:
predictive maintenance, generative design, and industrial automation. Each one solves a different kind of
problem, and seeing real examples will make the distinctions from the last lesson click into place.
Predictive Maintenance
The old way to maintain equipment is either reactive (fix it when it breaks) or scheduled (replace it on a fixed
calendar, whether it needs it or not). Both waste money  reactive maintenance means unplanned
downtime, and scheduled maintenance means replacing parts that still had useful life left. Predictive
maintenance uses machine learning to solve this: sensors continuously stream data off a machine, and the
model learns what "normal" looks like well enough to catch subtle signs of wear long before a human would
notice.
Three approaches to maintenance: reactive, scheduled, and predictive.
Siemens runs a system called Senseye that processes vibration, temperature, pressure, and power data
from manufacturing equipment, comparing it against historical baselines to catch things like early bearing
wear weeks before failure. Rolls-Royce does something similar in the air  its aircraft engines stream
thousands of data points per flight (exhaust temperature, blade vibration, oil pressure) back to ground
teams, so degradation can be caught mid-flight and replacement parts are already waiting when the plane
lands, instead of discovering a problem after touchdown.

Notice what kind of AI this actually is: it's machine learning, not simple rule-based logic. A rule-based
system could flag "temperature above X," but it can't learn the subtle combination of a dozen small
variances that, together, predict a bearing failure three weeks out. That pattern only shows up from data.
Generative Design
Generative design flips the usual design process. Instead of an engineer sketching a shape and then
checking whether it holds up under load, you give the AI the constraints  maximum dimensions, load
limits, material options, manufacturing method  and it generates thousands of possible structural
geometries that satisfy them, then narrows down to the most efficient ones.
The generative design process: constraints in, thousands of options generated, best design selected.
Airbus used this approach to redesign a cabin partition wall, letting the algorithm generate bio-inspired
lattice structures within the required safety and load constraints. The result was dramatically lighter than a
traditionally engineered part, without giving up structural strength  and weight savings on an aircraft

compound, because lighter parts mean less fuel burned over the aircraft's lifetime. General Motors used the

This is a different kind of AI use than predictive maintenance. It's not analyzing sensor data over time  it's
exploring a massive design space that would be impractical for a human engineer to search manually, and
returning options a person likely wouldn't have arrived at through conventional design instinct.
Industrial Automation
This is where AI moves from analysis into real-time control and decision-making on the factory floor. BMW
uses deep-learning computer vision along its assembly lines  cameras capture every vehicle chassis
moving down the line, and the model is trained to tell the difference between actual defects (a micro-crack,
a paint flaw) and harmless things like dust, at a speed and consistency no human inspector could match
doing the same job all day.

Siemens applies a related idea inside its own electronics manufacturing  at its Amberg plant, machine
learning models continuously adjust soldering speed and thermal profiles in real time based on humidity,
temperature, and small variations in incoming component batches, correcting the process before a defect
happens rather than catching it afterward.
Real-time process adjustment based on environmental sensor readings.
Foxconn takes it a step further, using AI-driven digital twins to coordinate an entire factory's robots,
conveyors, and workload balancing dynamically, instead of running on a fixed, pre-programmed sequence.

What ties these three together
Look back at the AI / ML / Deep Learning distinction from the last lesson, and you'll see all three of these
categories lean heavily on machine learning and deep learning specifically  not rule-based AI. That's not
a coincidence. The problems in this lesson (subtle sensor patterns, enormous design spaces, real-time
visual inspection) are exactly the kind of problems that are too complex to hand-code rules for, and exactly
the kind where learning from data outperforms a programmer's best guess at the rules.
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to look at a real AI application in an engineering context and
identify not just what it does, but which category of problem it's solving  is it forecasting a future state from
historical data, exploring a design space, or making real-time decisions from live sensor or visual input?
That framing will carry through the rest of this course.
